# Interim report: constant-celerity-routing-vs-process-chain-glof

**Locus question:** How wrong is a triangular/rectangular hydrograph at three constant wave speeds (2/4/6 m/s) against observed Himalayan GLOF/debris-flow events, and what minimal upgrade is justified?
**Flavor:** dialectical

## What the corpus already said
Mergili 2020 [[hess-reconstruction-of-the-1941-glof-process-chain-at-lake-palcacocha-cordillera]] back-calculates "travel times between 36 and 70 min to reach Huaraz, accompanied with peak discharges above 10 000 m3/s", with the caveat that "no travel times or velocities are documented for the 1941 event", so the calibration is itself model-derived. Tsho Rolpa NWS-BREACH [[piahs-glacial-lake-outburst-flood-glof-modeling-of-tsho-rolpa-glacial-lake-nepal]] gives source peaks of 8,198 m3/s (20 m breach) and 26,662 m3/s (40 m breach), with the latter cited 185 km downstream, i.e. quoted with no attenuation. Hoch 2019 [[nhess-evaluating-the-impact-of-model-complexity-on-flood-wave-propagation-and-in]] is a large-river study (kinematic wave vs local inertia, NSE 0.48 to 0.71), not a mountain-GLOF study. The Thame 2024 abstract [[abstract-egu25-12752]] says ~4-5x10^5 m3 of water was released, with "a complex pattern of erosion and deposition", and damage to a bridge ~45 km downstream. The Melamchi note [[the-2021-melamchi-flood-a-massive-erosional-cascade-in-the-himalayan-mountains-o]] says the widely distributed erosion suggests the event was "not primarily driven by a single source". No corpus note quantified celerity error.

## What the new sources say
- [[a-multi-source-quantitative-reconstruction-of-the-26-august-2026-bhote-koshi-roc]] (EarthArXiv preprint v1, NOT peer reviewed; author-reported synthesis of public data). Strongest new datum: "The source-to-border segment was reported at approximately 188 km/h (52.2 m/s), declining to 23-26 km/h in lower-river segments (6.4-7.2 m/s)", fitted with "a descriptive log-linear decay model at 80.1% of variation across six reported segment speeds". Discharge at Devghat rose 2,600 to 5,850 m3/s with a 19.96x10^6 m3 excess volume "34.8% of total flow volume"; channel width grew from a mean 66.1 to 266.5 m (22 sections). Arrival at Devghat is 365 min after the warning anchor. The abstract says the event was "rock-ice avalanche, progressive entrainment and debris-flow transformation, followed by downstream flood-wave routing". This is a flood that gained volume downstream, the opposite of attenuation.
- [[the-bhote-koshitrishuli-flash-flood-of-26-august-2026-rasuwa-nepal-warning-laten]] (preprint v5, single author). Front timed at six gauges from 10-min stage series: the uppermost gauge was reached "within 12.8 min", the public alert came 38.8 min after trigger, lead times were -26 to -16 min at the first settlement and +4 to +14 min at the next, and three gauges were destroyed at 27, 69 and 87% of their warning stages. Implication: in the upper reach the wave is faster than any 2-6 m/s setting, and arrival time, not magnitude, drives outcomes. Both preprints describe the same event and are not independent confirmation.
- [[abstract-egu25-768]] (South Lhonak 2023, conference abstract). Process chain: moraine creep failure, displacement wave, frontal moraine breach; Teesta III at Chungthang destroyed 63 km downstream; "transboundary effects extending hundreds of kilometers". The fetched abstract text I read gives no celerity figures; I did not verify them.
- Not fetched, honestly flagged: no observed celerity or attenuation data were obtained for Dig Tsho 1985, Gongbatongsha 2016 or Melamchi 2021 within budget. The Melamchi note had no arrival-time passage extractable by my search.

## Evidence synthesis
Sources agree on three things. (1) Himalayan GLOF fronts are not constant-speed: the one quantified event shows ~52 m/s near source decaying to ~6-7 m/s lower down, so the Atlas default (2/4/6 m/s) brackets only the lower-river part; applied from the source it would put near-source arrival late by up to an order of magnitude (e.g. first 10 km: ~3 min at 52 m/s vs 28-83 min at 6-2 m/s), which is non-conservative for the nearest, most exposed settlements. (2) Peak magnitude is not reliably attenuating: in entrainment-dominated cascades (Melamchi, Thame, Bhote Koshi) volume and peak grow or are re-supplied downstream (Bhote Koshi Devghat peak 5,850 m3/s with 35% excess volume), so a no-attenuation hydrograph is not obviously conservative or unconservative; it is simply uncorrelated with the physics. (3) The source hydrograph shape matters less than source physics: Mergili's 36-70 min range arises from varying process scenarios, and Tsho Rolpa's peak varies 3x with breach depth (20 vs 40 m).

Counter-evidence for the simple side: Mergili's range is itself usable as a travel-time bound, Hoch shows that physics upgrades (local inertia) improve peak NSE only moderately in large rivers, and the Bhote Koshi lower-river speeds (6-7 m/s) fall inside the Atlas's 2-6 m/s band. Quantification is thin: one event, preprint-grade, six segment speeds.

### Position A: constant-celerity routing is an acceptable communication-grade bound
Best case: the Atlas is a public scenario tool, not a forecast; it needs transparent, user-adjustable travel-time envelopes. Mergili's 36-70 min back-calculation and the 6-7 m/s lower-river speeds are consistent with a 2-6 m/s band at distance; process models need DEMs, rheology calibration and lake-specific data that a static site cannot supply (Hoch shows added complexity is not free of calibration burden).

### Position B: process chain dominates, constant celerity misleads
Best case: Melamchi, Thame, South Lhonak and Bhote Koshi 2026 were erosional or entrainment cascades; wave speed decays strongly with distance (52 to ~7 m/s), width grew 4.5x, and volume and peak grew downstream. A single constant celerity cannot reproduce either the near-field speed or the downstream peak, and the Bhote Koshi case shows what happens when timing is wrong: fatalities concentrated where lead time was negative.

## Committed position
Position B is right about the physics and Position A is right about the deliverable: keep the simple routing engine, but it is not defensible as is. The load-bearing reason is that the only quantified Himalayan celerity profile in the evidence shows a ~7x decay along the channel (52 m/s to 6-7 m/s), so any constant speed is wrong by several-fold in some reach, and the error is non-conservative close to the source. The minimal justified upgrade is: (1) replace constant celerity with a distance- or slope-dependent speed, e.g. v = v_near for the first 10-20 km (default 15-30 m/s for steep debris-flow-type reaches, flagged as preprint-derived) decaying log-linearly to 6-7 m/s, or equivalently keep the 2/4/6 m/s triplet but add an explicit "fast near-field" fourth case; (2) report arrival as an earliest-to-latest window rather than three lines; (3) do not apply attenuation to peak by default, because entrainment events show growth; show peak as source peak and label it "source value, downstream peak may be lower (attenuation in clear-water floods) or higher (entrainment)"; (4) add a visible label that the model is a clear-water, single-source breach with no entrainment, plus a process-chain warning (avalanche/landslide-triggered, small lakes such as Thame) that the tool does not cover those. Do not build full process-chain (r.avaflow-type) modelling in a static site.

- **Position:** Keep simple routing but make celerity distance-decaying with near-field fast case, report arrival windows, and label no-entrainment limits; do not add default peak attenuation.
- **Confidence:** medium (about 65%): direction is well supported; specific parameter values rest on one preprint event.
- **Boundary conditions:** holds for steep Himalayan river corridors for tens of km below a source; for broad lower reaches (>100 km, Devghat-type) 2-6 m/s is adequate. Clear-water moraine-dam breaches may behave differently (slower, attenuating).
- **What would change this position:** peer-reviewed measured celerities for Dig Tsho 1985, Gongbatongsha 2016, Melamchi 2021 or South Lhonak showing roughly constant 4-8 m/s from source to 60+ km; or retraction/correction of the Bhote Koshi preprints' 52 m/s figure (which may reflect avalanche/debris front, not flood wave, and is plausibly not a GLOF); or a documented empirical peak-attenuation relation fit to Himalayan GLOFs that would justify default attenuation.
- **Evidence weight:** 2 non-independent preprints on one 2026 event (supports B); 1 peer-reviewed back-calculation (Mergili, supports a bounded A); 1 large-river model comparison (weak relevance); 1 breach-peak study (source side only); 2 abstracts (Thame, South Lhonak) qualitative. No direct observed celerity for 2023 and 2024 events retrieved.

## Open questions
- Observed celerities and attenuation for Dig Tsho 1985 (Vuichard and Zimmermann), Gongbatongsha 2016, Melamchi 2021, Chungthang 2023, Thame 2024 gauge timing.
- Empirical Qp-vs-distance attenuation relations for GLOFs (e.g. Cenderelli and Wohl 2001, Huggel 2002) not fetched.
- Whether the 52 m/s near-source speed is a debris-front or a hydraulic wave, and its transferability to lake breaches.
